# Beyond Counterparty Counts — speaker notes

## Slide 1

This project starts from Egressy and colleagues' finding that port numbering improves AML graph learning. I ask whether ports still help when account-level counts are provided, whether a simple first-contact flag helps, and where the improvement appears. The main finding is straightforward: under our original protocol, replacing ports with the flag identifies more laundering on first-contact transfers. I will show the controlled comparisons and then a separate follow-up. All results refer to HI-Small.

## Slide 2

Before the model, the problem. Money laundering is the movement of illicit funds to hide where they came from. It is usually described in three stages: placement, when the money enters the financial system; layering, when it is moved around to obscure the trail; and integration, when it comes back out looking legitimate. The difficulty for monitoring is that an individual transfer can look ordinary: a normal amount, a normal payment type. The shape of the flows can provide evidence; legitimate activity can also form these patterns, so a shape is not itself proof of laundering. Four typical shapes are on screen. Fan-out: one account splits funds across many. Fan-in: many accounts funnel into one. Scatter-gather: funds are split and then recombined. And a cycle, where the money comes back to where it started. Fan-out and fan-in are defined by how many different counterparties are involved, and all four depend on who pays whom, not on how many transfers there are. So a detector has to be able to tell counterparties apart. Next: how a graph neural network reads this kind of data.

## Slide 3

So how does a graph neural network look for those shapes? I will describe exactly the model used in this project, which is called GIN. Step one: in this setup, accounts start blank. They carry no features of their own. All the information sits on the transfers: time, amount, currency and payment format. Step two is message passing. Every account builds a summary by adding up what arrives along its incoming transfers: each sender’s own summary, plus the fields of that transfer. We do this twice, so after two rounds an account’s summary reflects transfers up to two steps upstream. Step three: to score one transfer, here from u to v in rust, a small classifier reads three things: the summary of the sender, the summary of the receiver, and the transfer’s own fields. Out comes a laundering score. High scores go to a person for review; a score is not a finding of laundering. The phrase to remember for the next slide is “adding up”.

## Slide 4

Here is the weakness. Take account v, which receives three transfers in both pictures, so the number of transfers is the same. On the left, all three come from one sender. On the right, they come from three different senders, which is a fan-in. The network adds up the incoming messages. Because accounts start blank, a message is determined by the transfer’s fields. So when the three transfers look alike, v receives the same three messages in both cases, and their sums are identical: in an example like this, the network cannot tell one sender from three. That matters, because fan-in and fan-out are defined by the number of different counterparties. Egressy and colleagues proposed three adaptations to address this: reverse message passing, ego IDs and port numbering. Under the paper's theoretical assumptions, sufficiently expressive GNNs with all three adaptations can detect any directed subgraph pattern. This is not a guarantee about our particular two-layer trained model. On this benchmark, adding ports alone took the laundering-class F1 from about 29 to about 55, the largest single gain in that paper. The paper already notes that ports help a network count distinct neighbours, so that link is not my claim. Port numbering is next.

## Slide 5

Count means a historical total. We use two types on each side of a transfer: the number of earlier transfers and the number of earlier distinct counterparties. Port means a local label for a counterparty, assigned in first-contact order starting at zero. It is not a network-service port or a global account ID. In the table, S pays A, B, A, then C. Before the third transfer, S has sent two transfers to two receivers; A keeps port zero because S has paid A before. Before the fourth transfer, S has sent three transfers but has seen only two receivers, A and B. C is new, so it receives port two. This fourth row makes the difference between transfer count and distinct-counterparty count explicit. Counts summarize how much activity there has been; a port labels the particular counterparty involved now. We calculate counts before the current transaction in the fixed timestamp and tie order. Next, I show both sides of one transaction and all six feature values used by the models.

## Slide 6

For each current sender-to-receiver transfer, Counts supplies four numbers in this order: the sender's earlier outgoing transfer count, its earlier distinct-receiver count, the receiver's earlier incoming transfer count, and its earlier distinct-sender count. Ports supplies two local labels: the receiver's incoming label for this sender and the sender's outgoing label for this receiver. These are different numbering lists. In the worked history S to A, S to B, D to B, S to A, S has sent three times to two receivers. B has received twice from two senders. The next transfer S to B therefore has Counts [3,2,2,2]. S met A first and B second, so its outgoing port for B is one. B received from S first and D second, so its incoming port for S is zero. Ports are stored in incoming-then-outgoing order, [0,1], matching the code. Counts plus Ports supplies all six values, alongside the transaction's own fields. Novelty is a separate one-bit flag: one for the first observed directed pair and zero for a repeated pair; it is zero here. The raw port equals the corresponding earlier distinct count on first contacts and is smaller on repeats. These are raw values before normalization. With the feature definitions clear, I can now state the research questions.

## Slide 7

There are three steps in the argument. First: do ports still help when counts are provided? The relevant contrast is Counts plus Ports minus Counts. We also compare that residual gain with Ports minus Base, as in the original factorial design. Second: does a first-contact flag improve detection beyond counts? This compares Counts plus flag with Counts; neither arm has ports. The supplementary flag model is also compared with Counts plus Ports, establishing the replacement comparison that leads into the third question. Third: where are the extra detections when the flag replaces ports? Both models keep counts, and we describe the change in true-positive counts on first-contact and repeat transfers separately. This third question locates the observed detections; it does not identify a causal internal mechanism. These are not three newly declared primary tests: the flag model is supplementary and the error decomposition is descriptive. The completed follow-up that adds the flag while retaining ports is a separate comparison under a different protocol.

## Slide 8

HI-Small contains 5,078,345 transfers and 5,177 laundering labels. Accounts are nodes and transfers are directed edges. The whole-day cut points are six and eight. We use a two-layer edge-feature GIN and the fixed reference hyperparameters. The architecture and optimization settings are shared within each experiment, but the original input dimensions vary with the supplied features. Historical features use preceding transactions in a fixed timestamp order, with a label-independent permutation for equal timestamps; that is not strict earlier wall-clock time within a tie. Both graph evaluations are cumulative and transductive, so they are not online-causal backtests. Checkpoints and thresholds are chosen on validation. The original 25 runs use five epochs; the separate 15-run follow-up uses 20 to 30.

## Slide 9

The original experiment has a two-by-two design: Base, Counts, Ports and Counts plus Ports. The four counts are earlier outgoing transfer and distinct-receiver counts for the sender, and earlier incoming transfer and distinct-sender counts for the receiver. We compare the port gain without and with these counts. The original named estimand is one minus residual port gain divided by the port gain without counts; the additive contrasts make it easier to interpret. Counts plus flag is supplementary. Each model has five seeds and a five-epoch budget. The original protocol normalizes separately in each cumulative split graph and leaves dropout and batch statistics active at scoring. Its checkpoints maximize validation F1 over the declared eligible epochs. This is a fixed-budget, transductive comparison; the intervals quantify training and scoring seed variation, not uncertainty across banks. The supplementary intervals are unadjusted. The later protocol changes these evaluation choices and is reported separately.

## Slide 10

Do ports still help when counts are provided? Yes, in this original protocol. The right-hand pair compares Counts plus Ports, at mean F1 39.57, with Counts, at 24.66. Adding ports therefore improves F1 by 14.91 points, with interval 10.11 to 19.71; every paired seed has a positive difference. The left-hand pair is the reference port gain without counts: 7.48 points. The 14.91 is the effect of adding ports to a model with counts, not the effect of adding counts. A direct replacement question would instead compare Counts with Ports, which is a different contrast. Our conclusion is that ports retain predictive value after the four counts are supplied. This does not establish that counts are useless or that the two feature sets have a statistically significant interaction; the interaction interval crosses zero. The original planned share-explained Fieller interval remains minus 512.7 to plus 8.4 percent, conditional on this protocol and statistical model. Next, what information do the count scalars leave out?

## Slide 11

The account counts can be identical even when the current pair is new in one case and repeated in another. Here u has paid a and b, while d has paid c. The three possible next transfers from u have identical count vectors but different ports. Comparing a port with its corresponding earlier distinct count determines whether the pair is new. With d for the distinct count and p for the zero-based port, d minus p is zero for a first contact and positive for a repeat. On repeats, d minus p minus one counts counterparties first encountered after this relationship. That explains the temporal meaning of the encoding; it does not prove which computation the trained classifier uses. We test the simple first-contact bit as a supplementary input. Does adding this flag improve detection beyond counts alone?

## Slide 12

Does a first-contact flag improve detection beyond counts? In the original experiment, yes. To answer this, compare Counts plus flag with Counts. Neither model has ports. Their mean F1 scores are 48.99 and 24.66, so adding the flag improves F1 by 24.33 points, with a nominal paired interval of 21.47 to 27.19. The benefit remains positive on days eight and nine and on average precision. This flag model is supplementary. The same figure provides a second, distinct comparison: Counts plus flag versus Counts plus Ports, whose mean F1 is 39.57. Keeping counts and replacing ports with the flag improves F1 by 9.42 points, with interval 5.73 to 13.11. That replacement comparison leads to Question 3: where are the extra detections when the flag replaces ports? The later follow-up instead keeps ports and adds the flag; it is a separate question.

## Slide 13

Where are the extra detections when the flag replaces ports? They are on first-contact transfers. Both models retain the four counts; the comparison is Counts plus flag minus Counts plus Ports, rather than Counts plus flag minus Counts. On first-contact transfers, Counts plus Ports finds 623.8 laundering transactions on average; Counts plus flag finds 848.4. Recall rises from 47.08 to 64.03 percent, an increase of 16.95 percentage points. Every seed detects more first-contact positives. First-contact F1 rises by 11.40 points, with a nominal interval of 7.54 to 15.26. The right panel locates the net increase: first-contact true positives increase by 224.6, while repeat true positives decrease by 7.4, giving a total net increase of 217.2. These are mean count differences, not the number of newly discovered cases or a decomposition of F1. First-contact false alerts average 903.2 versus 903.4, but the individual changes range from minus 435 to plus 506, and thresholds differ. We do not claim equal workload or proven unchanged false positives. The repeat-pair F1 difference is minus 4.05 with interval minus 12.03 to plus 3.93, so no separate repeat benefit is established. This is descriptive localization of the observed detections, not proof of an internal causal mechanism. One completed follow-up checks whether the flag also helps when ports are retained.

## Slide 14

This completed follow-up asks a different question: does an explicit flag help when counts and ports are both retained? Raw counts and ports already determine the flag, so this tests an explicit representation of available information, not added information. All arms have eleven slots, matched initialization and parameter count, training-only shared count-port scaling, and evaluation-mode scoring. Runs use new seeds fifteen to nineteen and twenty to thirty epochs with validation stopping. The exact highest validation-AP checkpoint is selected, followed by a validation-F1 threshold. The paired F1 change is minus 7.21 points with interval minus 15.46 to plus 1.03; AP is minus 4.49 with interval minus 19.11 to plus 10.13. A positive addition benefit is not established, nor are harm or equivalence proved. Nine of fifteen runs reached the cap, so stopping is not convergence. This protocol has no Counts-only arm and cannot directly re-test the plus 24.33 contrast. Its third arm, Counts plus flag, is included in the full report, and its secondary contrasts are unresolved. The protocol changed several things, so differences between studies cannot be attributed only to training duration. I now return to the positive findings established in the original experiment.

## Slide 15

I close with three concrete findings. First, account-history counts do not remove the port benefit. After all four counts are supplied, ports still improve F1 by 14.91 points. Second, under the original experimental protocol, a first-contact flag has clear predictive value. Adding it to Counts improves F1 by 24.33 points, and replacing ports with the flag, while retaining counts, improves F1 by 9.42 points. Third, we locate the additional detections: laundering recall on first-contact transfers rises from 47.1 to 64.0 percent, and every one of the five seeds detects more of these laundering transfers. Our research contribution is to distinguish account-level activity from the history of the specific account pair through controlled feature comparisons, and to quantify both the predictive value of a simple relationship flag and the transaction group where detection improves. For AML feature design, the implication is to consider whether the current sender has previously paid this receiver alongside cumulative transaction and counterparty counts. This study provides evidence for that relationship feature on the current benchmark and protocol; the observed patterns are not proof of laundering by themselves. The flag comparison is supplementary, the detection breakdown is descriptive, and the completed follow-up did not establish an additional benefit when ports were retained. These boundaries do not change the three reported findings. Thank you; I welcome questions.
