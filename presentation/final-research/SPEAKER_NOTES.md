# Beyond Counterparty Counts — speaker notes

15 slides, including cover and references. No backup section.

## Slide 1

This project starts from Egressy and colleagues' finding that port numbering improves AML graph learning. I ask whether account-level counts can substitute for ports, whether a simple first-contact flag helps, and where the improvement appears. The main finding is straightforward: under our original protocol, replacing ports with the flag identifies more laundering on first-contact transfers. I will show the controlled comparisons and then a separate follow-up. All results refer to HI-Small.

## Slide 2

Before the model, the problem. Money laundering is the movement of illicit funds to hide where they came from. It is usually described in three stages: placement, when the money enters the financial system; layering, when it is moved around to obscure the trail; and integration, when it comes back out looking legitimate. The difficulty for monitoring is that an individual transfer can look ordinary: a normal amount, a normal payment type. The shape of the flows can provide evidence; legitimate activity can also form these patterns, so a shape is not itself proof of laundering. Four typical shapes are on screen. Fan-out: one account splits funds across many. Fan-in: many accounts funnel into one. Scatter-gather: funds are split and then recombined. And a cycle, where the money comes back to where it started. Fan-out and fan-in are defined by how many different counterparties are involved, and all four depend on who pays whom, not on how many transfers there are. So a detector has to be able to tell counterparties apart. Next: how a graph neural network reads this kind of data.

## Slide 3

So how does a graph neural network look for those shapes? I will describe exactly the model used in this project, which is called GIN. Step one: in this setup, accounts start blank. They carry no features of their own. All the information sits on the transfers: time, amount, currency and payment format. Step two is message passing. Every account builds a summary by adding up what arrives along its incoming transfers: each sender’s own summary, plus the fields of that transfer. We do this twice, so after two rounds an account’s summary reflects transfers up to two steps upstream. Step three: to score one transfer, here from u to v in rust, a small classifier reads three things: the summary of the sender, the summary of the receiver, and the transfer’s own fields. Out comes a laundering score. High scores go to a person for review; a score is not a finding of laundering. The phrase to remember for the next slide is “adding up”.

## Slide 4

Here is the weakness. Take account v, which receives three transfers in both pictures, so the number of transfers is the same. On the left, all three come from one sender. On the right, they come from three different senders, which is a fan-in. The network adds up the incoming messages. Because accounts start blank, a message is determined by the transfer’s fields. So when the three transfers look alike, v receives the same three messages in both cases, and their sums are identical: in an example like this, the network cannot tell one sender from three. That matters, because fan-in and fan-out are defined by the number of different counterparties. Egressy and colleagues proposed three adaptations to address this: reverse message passing, ego IDs and port numbering. Under the paper's theoretical assumptions, sufficiently expressive GNNs with all three adaptations can detect any directed subgraph pattern. This is not a guarantee about our particular two-layer trained model. On this benchmark, adding ports alone took the laundering-class F1 from about 29 to about 55, the largest single gain in that paper. The paper already notes that ports help a network count distinct neighbours, so that link is not my claim. Port numbering is next.

## Slide 5

Ports are local labels assigned in first-contact order. In the example, S pays A, then B, then A again, so the outgoing ports are zero, one, zero. Before each transfer, S has seen zero, one, two distinct receivers. A first contact has port equal to the previous distinct count; a repeat has a smaller port. The receiver has the corresponding incoming count and port. First contact means first observed within this dataset window, not necessarily the first interaction in real banking history. Egressy et al. already motivate the use of timestamps for port ordering. Our question is how useful a simple property of that history is in this task.

## Slide 6

There are three steps in the argument. First, test whether four historical account counts remove the port gain; this is the original factorial question. Second, evaluate a supplementary first-contact flag and compare it with both Counts and Counts plus Ports. Third, describe which transfers account for the extra detections. These are not three newly declared primary tests: the flag model is supplementary and the error decomposition is descriptive. I also retain the completed follow-up that adds the flag while keeping ports, under a different protocol.

## Slide 7

HI-Small contains 5,078,345 transfers and 5,177 laundering labels. Accounts are nodes and transfers are directed edges. The whole-day cut points are six and eight. We use a two-layer edge-feature GIN and the fixed reference hyperparameters. The architecture and optimization settings are shared within each experiment, but the original input dimensions vary with the supplied features. Historical features use preceding transactions in a fixed timestamp order, with a label-independent permutation for equal timestamps; that is not strict earlier wall-clock time within a tie. Both graph evaluations are cumulative and transductive, so they are not online-causal backtests. Checkpoints and thresholds are chosen on validation. The original 25 runs use five epochs; the separate 15-run follow-up uses 20 to 30.

## Slide 8

The original experiment has a two-by-two design: Base, Counts, Ports and Counts plus Ports. The four counts are earlier outgoing transfer and distinct-receiver counts for the sender, and earlier incoming transfer and distinct-sender counts for the receiver. We compare the port gain without and with these counts. The original named estimand is one minus residual port gain divided by the port gain without counts; the additive contrasts make it easier to interpret. Counts plus flag is supplementary. Each model has five seeds and a five-epoch budget. The original protocol normalizes separately in each cumulative split graph and leaves dropout and batch statistics active at scoring. Its checkpoints maximize validation F1 over the declared eligible epochs. This is a fixed-budget, transductive comparison; the intervals quantify training and scoring seed variation, not uncertainty across banks. The supplementary intervals are unadjusted. The later protocol changes these evaluation choices and is reported separately.

## Slide 9

Do counts replace ports? No, in this original protocol. Ports add 7.48 F1 points without the counts and 14.91 with them. The residual interval is 10.11 to 19.71, positive in every paired seed. Supplying these four counts therefore does not remove the port benefit. I am not claiming the gain was statistically shown to grow; the interaction interval crosses zero. The planned share-explained Fieller interval is minus 512.7 to plus 8.4 percent, conditional on this protocol and statistical model. Counts alone has a small point estimate over Base but a wide interval, so this is not proof that counts are useless. Next, what information do the count scalars leave out?

## Slide 10

The account counts can be identical even when the current pair is new in one case and repeated in another. Here u has paid a and b, while d has paid c. The three possible next transfers from u have identical count vectors but different ports. Comparing a port with its corresponding earlier distinct count determines whether the pair is new. With d for the distinct count and p for the zero-based port, d minus p is zero for a first contact and positive for a repeat. On repeats, d minus p minus one counts counterparties first encountered after this relationship. That explains the temporal meaning of the encoding; it does not prove which computation the trained classifier uses. We test the simple first-contact bit as a supplementary input. How much does it help?

## Slide 11

Does a first-contact flag help? In the original experiment, yes. Counts plus flag reaches 48.99 mean F1, compared with 24.66 for Counts and 39.57 for Counts plus Ports. The addition to Counts gives 24.33 points, with a nominal paired interval of 21.47 to 27.19. The benefit remains positive on days eight and nine and on average precision. The flag model is supplementary. It also exceeds Counts plus Ports by 9.42 points, but this swaps the port representation for the flag; it is not evidence that adding the flag on top of ports helps. The practical question now is clearer: when the flag replaces ports, which transactions account for the extra detections?

## Slide 12

Where does the gain appear? On first-contact transfers, Counts plus Ports finds 623.8 laundering transactions on average; Counts plus flag finds 848.4. Recall rises from 47.08 to 64.03 percent, an increase of 16.95 percentage points. Every seed detects more first-contact positives. First-contact F1 rises by 11.40 points, with a nominal interval of 7.54 to 15.26. The right panel locates the net increase: first-contact true positives increase by 224.6, while repeat true positives decrease by 7.4, giving a total net increase of 217.2. These are mean count differences, not the number of newly discovered cases or a decomposition of F1. First-contact false alerts average 903.2 versus 903.4, but the individual changes range from minus 435 to plus 506, and thresholds differ. We do not claim equal workload or proven unchanged false positives. The repeat-pair F1 difference is minus 4.05 with interval minus 12.03 to plus 3.93, so no separate repeat benefit is established. This is descriptive localization of the observed prediction gain, not proof of an internal causal mechanism. One completed follow-up checks whether the flag also helps when ports are retained.

## Slide 13

This completed follow-up asks a different question: does an explicit flag help when counts and ports are both retained? Raw counts and ports already determine the flag, so this tests an explicit representation of available information, not added information. All arms have eleven slots, matched initialization and parameter count, training-only shared count-port scaling, and evaluation-mode scoring. Runs use new seeds fifteen to nineteen and twenty to thirty epochs with validation stopping. The exact highest validation-AP checkpoint is selected, followed by a validation-F1 threshold. The paired F1 change is minus 7.21 points with interval minus 15.46 to plus 1.03; AP is minus 4.49 with interval minus 19.11 to plus 10.13. A positive addition benefit is not established, nor are harm or equivalence proved. Nine of fifteen runs reached the cap, so stopping is not convergence. This protocol has no Counts-only arm and cannot directly re-test the plus 24.33 contrast. Its third arm, Counts plus flag, is included in the full report, and its secondary contrasts are unresolved. The protocol changed several things, so differences between studies cannot be attributed only to training duration. I now return to the positive findings established in the original experiment.

## Slide 14

The main finding is simple: in the original experiment, a first-contact flag helps find laundering on new pairs. First, the four account-level counts do not remove the port benefit: 14.91 F1 points remain. Second, the flag is useful beyond counts, with a 24.33-point gain, and replacing ports with the flag gives a 9.42-point gain. Third, we locate the extra detections: first-contact recall rises from 47.1 to 64.0 percent, and all five seeds detect more first-contact positives. These are results from the original five-epoch HI-Small protocol, with the flag comparison supplementary. The follow-up has already shown that an additional benefit on top of retained ports was not established. The practical research contribution is to evaluate pair-specific history alongside account-level activity counts, and to show which transactions account for the observed prediction gain. Thank you; I welcome questions.
